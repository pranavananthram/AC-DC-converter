# Adjustable AC-DC Converter

## Overview

This project involved the design, simulation, and physical implementation of an adjustable AC-DC converter capable of converting a ±7.5 VAC input into a regulated DC output adjustable between approximately 10 V and 20 V.

The system integrates three primary circuit stages: a full-wave bridge rectifier with capacitive filtering, a 555 timer configured in astable mode, and a MOSFET-based boost converter. An output regulation stage consisting of Zener diode branches and a potentiometer provides adjustable output voltage control.

The circuit was first developed and validated using NI Multisim before being constructed and tested on a physical breadboard. Simulation results were compared against oscilloscope measurements to evaluate circuit performance and identify differences between theoretical and practical implementation.

## Project Objectives

The primary objective was to develop an adjustable DC power supply meeting the following design specifications:

| Parameter | Design Specification |
|---|---|
| Input voltage | ±7.5 VAC |
| Output voltage | +10 V to +20 V DC |
| Maximum output current | 1.0 mA |
| Rectifier ripple | Less than 1.0 V |
| Output ripple | Less than 100 mV |
| Voltage adjustment | Single potentiometer |

The design emphasizes AC rectification, voltage conversion, switching control, output regulation, and practical circuit validation.

## System Architecture

The converter consists of three main functional stages and an output regulation circuit.

### 1. Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier

The input stage converts the AC signal supplied by a laboratory transformer into an unregulated DC voltage.

A full-wave bridge rectifier and capacitive filter were implemented to:
- Convert the alternating input signal into DC.
- Reduce voltage fluctuations through capacitive filtering.
- Provide a DC supply for the 555 timer and boost converter.

**Simulation Results:**
- Rectifier output: approximately 8.6 V DC.
- Output ripple: approximately 0.2 V.

**Physical Measurements:**
- Rectifier output: approximately 8.8 V DC.
- Output ripple: approximately 0.165 V.

The rectifier successfully met the requirement of maintaining ripple below 1 V.

### 2. 555 Timer Oscillator

A 555 timer configured in astable mode generates the periodic square-wave signal used to control the switching stage of the boost converter.

External resistors and capacitors determine the operating frequency and duty cycle.

The timer's frequency and duty cycle were initially calculated using:

Frequency:

f = 1.44 / ((R1 + 2R2) × C)

Duty cycle:

D = (R1 + R2) / (R1 + 2R2) × 100%

The initial Multisim configuration used:
- R1 = 3 kΩ
- R2 = 1 kΩ
- C6 = 0.01 µF

This configuration produced a simulated frequency of approximately 13 kHz and an 80% duty cycle.

During physical implementation, the timer configuration required modification to improve boost converter operation and prevent component failures.

The final physical configuration used:
- R1 = 100 kΩ
- R2 = 100 kΩ
- C4 = 33 pF
- C6 = 33 pF

The resulting physical output operated at approximately 71 kHz with a 71% duty cycle.

### 3. Boost Converter

The boost converter increases the rectified DC input voltage using a transistor-switched inductor topology.

The primary components include:
- Switching MOSFET.
- Inductor.
- Catch diode.
- Output capacitor.
- Switching signal generated by the 555 timer (with a function generator also used during development).

The switching operation stores energy in the inductor and transfers it to the output stage, allowing the converter to produce a voltage greater than its input voltage.

**Simulation Results:**
- Input voltage: approximately 8.6 V DC.
- Boosted output voltage: approximately 100 V DC.

**Physical Measurements:**
- Boosted output voltage: approximately 20.8 V DC.
- Output ripple: approximately 0.5 V.

Although the simulated output was substantially higher than the physical output, the physical implementation produced sufficient voltage for the intended 10–20 V regulation stage.

### 4. Output Voltage Regulation

The final stage uses two parallel diode branches and a potentiometer to provide adjustable output voltage control.

Zener diodes establish voltage thresholds, while forward-biased 1N4007G diodes contribute additional voltage drops.

The original design included:

**Higher-voltage branch:**
- 1 × 1N4744A Zener diode (15 V).
- 1 × 1N4732A Zener diode (4.7 V).
- 1 × 1N4007G diode (approximately 0.7 V).

**Lower-voltage branch:**
- 2 × 1N4732A Zener diodes (4.7 V each).
- 1 × 1N4007G diode (approximately 0.7 V).

The branches were subsequently modified during physical testing to improve the regulation range.

The final configurations included:
- Lower-voltage branch: two 1N4732A Zener diodes and three 1N4007G diodes.
- Higher-voltage branch: one 1N4744A Zener diode, one 1N4732A Zener diode, and four 1N4007G diodes.

A potentiometer was incorporated into the lower-voltage branch to allow adjustment of the output voltage.

The final circuit was evaluated under different load and potentiometer configurations.

**Maximum output voltage:**
- Load resistance: 20 kΩ.
- Potentiometer resistance: 50 kΩ.
- Physical output voltage: 19.408 V.
- Measured ripple: 0.241 V.

**Minimum output voltage:**
- Load resistance: 10 kΩ.
- Potentiometer resistance: 0 Ω.

## Engineering Challenges and Troubleshooting

The transition from simulation to physical implementation introduced several challenges that required iterative circuit modifications.

### MOSFET and Diode Failures

During initial breadboard testing, the boost converter failed to operate correctly, resulting in a damaged MOSFET and multiple damaged diodes.

The switching configuration was subsequently revised, including adjustments to the 555 timer's operating frequency and duty cycle.

### Simulation-to-Hardware Differences

The Multisim boost converter produced approximately 100 V, while the physical implementation reached approximately 20.8 V.

This highlighted the differences between simulated circuit behavior and practical hardware implementation.

Component behavior, circuit assembly, and operating conditions required additional consideration during physical testing.

### Output Ripple

Although the initial circuit achieved an output ripple below 100 mV, subsequent hardware failures and circuit modifications resulted in increased ripple.

The final integrated circuit exhibited ripple measurements ranging from approximately 197 mV to 900 mV, depending on the operating conditions.

The original ripple specification was therefore not consistently achieved.

### Design Improvements

Potential improvements identified during the project include:

- Further optimization of the 555 timer frequency and duty cycle.
- Experimentation with boost converter capacitor values to improve output filtering.
- Investigation of switching behavior and component selection.
- Further refinement of the Zener diode regulation branches.
- Additional testing under different load conditions.

These iterations reinforced the importance of validating simulated designs through physical measurements and systematically investigating discrepancies.

## Project Takeaways

This project provided practical experience in designing, simulating, constructing, and troubleshooting an AC-DC power conversion system.

Key engineering concepts explored included:
- AC-to-DC conversion using full-wave rectification.
- Capacitive filtering and ripple reduction.
- Astable oscillator design and timing calculations.
- MOSFET-based switching and boost converter operation.
- Zener diode voltage regulation.
- Simulation validation against physical measurements.
- Hardware debugging and iterative circuit optimization.

One of the most important lessons was understanding the limitations of simulation when transitioning to physical hardware. Differences between simulated and measured behavior required changes to component values, switching parameters, and circuit configurations.
